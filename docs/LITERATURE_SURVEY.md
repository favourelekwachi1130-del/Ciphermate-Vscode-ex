AI-Augmented Static Analysis for Vulnerability Detection and Developer Remediation Efficiency


The preceding abstract established that this thesis evaluates the impact of AI augmentation on static analysis along two dimensions: vulnerability detection accuracy and developer remediation efficiency. The research is grounded in CipherMate, an AI-augmented security extension for Visual Studio Code that integrates multiple static analyzers—dependency scanning, secrets detection, code-pattern analysis, and smart-contract checking—with a conversational AI layer for context-aware filtering, exploitability scoring, and remediation generation. The abstract framed the work within cybersecurity objectives: reducing exploitable vulnerabilities, improving prioritisation by real-world risk, and accelerating time-to-patch.

This literature survey situates that work within the broader research landscape. It examines three interconnected strands: (1) the effectiveness and adoption barriers of static analysis tools, which motivate the need for augmentation; (2) AI and machine learning approaches for vulnerability detection, which provide both inspiration and cautionary lessons; and (3) developer experience with security tooling, which informs how augmentation must be integrated into workflows. The survey identifies key contributions, critiques their limitations, and explains how CipherMate builds upon and extends this prior work. Emphasis is placed on peer-reviewed sources from top-tier venues—ICSE, IEEE S&P, NDSS, ACM, and comparable journals—to ensure the foundations for this research are sound.


Static Application Security Testing (SAST) tools have been a cornerstone of secure development for decades, yet their adoption and effectiveness remain contested. Bessey et al. (2010) provided an early, influential account of how static analysis matured from academic prototypes to industrial deployment. In "A few billion lines of code later: How static analysis has matured," published in Communications of the ACM, they described the evolution of commercial tools such as Coverity—used to analyse billions of lines of code at major technology firms—and the engineering challenges of scaling analysis to large, heterogeneous codebases. They identified three primary constraints: precision (the need to minimise false positives lest developers lose trust), performance (analysis must complete within acceptable time bounds), and usability (findings must be presented in a way that developers can act upon). Perhaps most importantly, they argued that static analysis had "crossed the chasm" from research curiosity to production tooling only by relentlessly focussing on these three dimensions. This paper establishes a baseline understanding of why static analysis, despite its theoretical promise for finding bugs automatically, has struggled to achieve widespread, sustained adoption: tools that violate any of these constraints are abandoned or ignored. CipherMate's design reflects these lessons: it uses established analyzers (Semgrep, Bandit) for performance and precision, and adds an AI layer specifically to improve usability—filtering, explaining, and remediating—thereby addressing the constraint that Bessey et al. identified as often underemphasised.

Johnson et al. (2013) offer one of the most frequently cited empirical studies on this topic. In "Why don't software developers use static analysis tools to find bugs?," presented at the International Conference on Software Engineering (ICSE)—a Tier-1 venue in software engineering—they interviewed 20 developers to identify barriers to adoption. Their findings are directly relevant to the present research. They found that false positives were the dominant barrier: developers lose trust when tools report many non-actionable findings, leading them to disable checks or ignore warnings entirely. Equally important was what they termed "warning presentation"—the manner in which defects are communicated. Developers reported that unclear messages, lack of prioritisation, and absence of remediation guidance made it difficult to act on findings even when those findings were valid. The authors noted that tools often provide rule identifiers and file locations but little context on why a finding matters or how to fix it. Johnson et al. recommended interactive mechanisms to help developers understand and fix defects, suggesting that improved tool design around result presentation could significantly increase adoption. This work motivates the hypothesis that AI augmentation—by filtering false positives, providing contextual explanation, and generating remediation—could address the very barriers Johnson et al. identified. CipherMate builds directly on this by using an AI layer to filter findings (reducing false positive burden), score exploitability (improving prioritisation), and generate fix suggestions with explanations (addressing the remediation gap), thereby responding to the "warning presentation" problem Johnson et al. emphasised. The thesis will evaluate whether these design choices measurably improve developer engagement with static analysis output.

Christakis and Bird (2016) extended this line of inquiry in "What developers want and need from program analysis: An empirical study," presented at the Automated Software Engineering (ASE) conference. Through a multi-method approach combining interviews and surveys at Microsoft, they identified four categories of developer needs: barriers to adoption (including poorly expressed warnings and distrust of results), desired functionality (types of issues developers care about), non-functional characteristics (workflow fit and reporting), and real-world defect analysis. They also investigated actual defects in Microsoft software to determine which code issues are most prevalent and impactful. A critical insight was that developers value actionable, well-prioritised findings over sheer volume; a long list of uncategorised warnings was often less useful than a short, focused list of high-confidence issues. Their work reinforces the case for AI-augmented triage: if tools could automatically distinguish true from false positives, cluster related findings, and rank by exploitability or business impact, developers might engage more meaningfully with static analysis. CipherMate's context-aware filtering, semantic deduplication, exploitability scoring, and natural-language Q&A ("what should I fix first?") are designed to fulfil precisely these needs. The thesis will assess whether developers using CipherMate report higher satisfaction and faster remediation than when using unaugmented analyzers.

Sadowski et al. (2018) provided an industrial perspective in "Lessons from building static analysis tools at Google," published in Communications of the ACM. Drawing on Google's experience deploying static analysis to thousands of engineers analysing billions of lines of code, they identified six reasons engineers avoid or ignore warnings: (1) tools not integrated into workflow or running too slowly; (2) lack of actionability—warnings that do not suggest clear next steps; (3) distrust due to false positives; (4) bugs that do not manifest in practice; (5) high cost or risk of fixing; and (6) unclear warnings. Their recommendations were threefold: focus on the developer and listen to feedback, integrate carefully into workflow so analysis runs where and when developers work, and crowdsource analysis development to scale rule coverage. These lessons inform the design of CipherMate. The extension is embedded in the IDE (Visual Studio Code) where developers already work, avoiding the friction of switching to external tools. It provides conversational access ("what should I fix first?", "explain the critical ones") and AI-generated remediation to increase actionability. It aims to reduce distrust by filtering findings and providing explanations that help developers judge whether a finding is worth addressing. CipherMate thus applies Sadowski et al.'s lessons in a different context: a general-purpose IDE extension available to any developer, rather than an internal Google infrastructure. The thesis will evaluate whether this embedding and conversational interface improves adoption and remediation rates compared to standalone static analysis tools.

Beller et al. (2016) quantified the state of static analysis in open source in "Analyzing the state of static analysis: A large-scale evaluation in open source software," presented at SANER. Analysing nine Automated Static Analysis Tools (ASATs)—including FindBugs and JSHint—across 122 popular projects and 168,214 open-source projects overall, they found that approximately half of state-of-the-art projects employ at least one ASAT, but typically in an ad hoc fashion. A significant finding was that most configurations deviate only slightly from defaults; developers rarely introduce custom rules or analyses. Furthermore, once configured, ASAT settings tend to remain static, with changes—when they occur—happening shortly after initial setup. To enable cross-tool comparison, the authors introduced a General Defect Classification (GDC) mapping 1,825 ASAT-specific warnings to 16 top-level classes. This work suggests that developers adopt tools when integration is simple but rarely customise them deeply—a finding that supports the value of tools that "work out of the box" with sensible defaults. CipherMate's multi-scanner orchestration with AI-driven scan planning (suggesting which scanners to run based on project structure and language) aligns with this: it reduces configuration burden while maintaining broad coverage across dependency, secrets, and code-pattern analysis.

Muske and Serebrenik (2017), in "Survey of approaches for handling static analysis alarms," also published at SANER, systematically reviewed techniques for reducing and managing static analysis alarms. They categorised approaches into filtering, ranking, clustering, and fix generation. Their survey highlighted that purely syntactic or rule-based filtering has limitations, and that semantic or learning-based approaches show promise. This provides a conceptual foundation for AI augmentation: the problems of alarm management are well understood, and the gap is in applying modern AI effectively. CipherMate contributes by implementing an end-to-end AI augmentation pipeline—filtering, prioritisation, clustering, and remediation—in a single, integrated tool.

 AI and Machine Learning for Vulnerability Detection

The application of AI and machine learning to vulnerability detection has grown rapidly, with both encouraging results and critical reassessments.

Li et al. (2018) introduced VulDeePecker in "A deep learning-based system for vulnerability detection," presented at the Network and Distributed System Security (NDSS) Symposium, a Tier-1 security conference. VulDeePecker represented a significant step toward applying deep learning to vulnerability detection at scale. The authors introduced the concept of "code gadgets"—semantic units extracted from source code that capture data and control flow relevant to vulnerabilities—and trained a bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) network to classify gadgets as vulnerable or benign. On the NVD and SARD datasets, VulDeePecker achieved accuracy in excess of 90% for certain vulnerability types, demonstrating that learned representations could capture patterns that rule-based tools might miss. However, VulDeePecker was limited to C/C++ and to vulnerabilities involving library/API function calls; generalisation to other languages and vulnerability classes remained an open question. Its contribution is methodological: it established that neural models can learn from code structure for security tasks and that gadget-based representation is viable. CipherMate does not replicate VulDeePecker's model architecture, but it inherits the insight that AI can augment rule-based analysis. Where VulDeePecker learns to detect vulnerabilities from scratch, CipherMate uses AI to post-process existing static analysis output—filtering, prioritising, and explaining—which may offer better robustness across languages, frameworks, and vulnerability types while retaining the interpretability of rule-based detection.

Zhou et al. (2019) extended this direction in "Devign: Effective vulnerability identification by learning comprehensive program semantics," presented at NeurIPS. Devign addressed a limitation of sequence-based approaches (such as VulDeePecker) by constructing a joint code representation from abstract syntax trees (ASTs), control flow graphs (CFGs), and data flow graphs (DFGs). The authors built a graph neural network that learned over these combined structures, capturing both syntactic and semantic relationships. On a real-world dataset derived from the Linux kernel and the QEMU project—consisting of thousands of patched and unpatched C functions—Devign achieved accuracy exceeding 87%, outperforming prior learning-based methods. A key contribution was demonstrating that richer code representations (property graphs) yield better vulnerability identification than sequences or ASTs alone. Devign's design, however, required significant preprocessing and was evaluated primarily on C. CipherMate takes a complementary approach: rather than replacing static analysis with a learned detector, it augments existing analyzers—which already implement rich semantic analysis—with AI for triage, prioritisation, and remediation. This hybrid design may offer better generalisability across languages (CipherMate supports JavaScript, TypeScript, Python, and others via Semgrep and Bandit) and greater interpretability, since the underlying detection logic remains rule-based and auditable.

A critical counterpoint is provided by Chakraborty et al. (2021) in "Deep learning based vulnerability detection: Are we there yet?," published in IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, a leading journal in software engineering (impact factor approximately 6.5). The authors subjected state-of-the-art deep learning approaches—including VulDeePecker and Devign—to rigorous evaluation under conditions designed to expose common methodological flaws. They identified three major problems: (1) data leakage through duplication of vulnerable and non-vulnerable code across train and test sets, which inflated reported accuracy; (2) unrealistic class distributions that did not reflect real-world prevalence of vulnerabilities; and (3) artifact learning—models learning to recognise dataset-specific cues (e.g., particular variable or function names) rather than genuine vulnerability semantics. When these issues were corrected, performance dropped by more than 50% in many cases. The authors proposed a more principled methodology and demonstrated that with careful data curation and model design, precision could be improved by 33.57% and recall by 128.38% over baseline approaches. This paper is essential reading for any research applying AI to vulnerability detection: it cautions against overclaiming and underscores the importance of evaluation rigour. CipherMate responds by not relying on learned detection as the primary mechanism; instead, it uses AI for tasks where the failure modes differ—filtering false positives, explaining findings, and generating remediation—and where the output is always subject to human review. The static analyzers (Semgrep, Bandit, retire.js, etc.) provide detection; the AI layer assists with interpretation and action. This design aligns with Chakraborty et al.'s implied recommendation: use AI where it adds value without replacing well-understood, auditable detection logic.

Allamanis et al. (2018), in "A survey of machine learning for big code and naturalness," published in ACM Computing Surveys (a leading venue for systematic reviews in computing), provided a comprehensive overview of applying machine learning to source code. The survey covered representation learning (e.g., word embeddings for code, graph-based representations), program synthesis and completion, defect and vulnerability prediction, and the broader concept of "naturalness" of software—the observation that code, like natural language, exhibits statistical regularities that can be captured and exploited by machine learning. The authors positioned VulDeePecker, Devign, and related work within this landscape, noting both the promise and the challenges of learned approaches. For the present research, their survey reinforces that the field is highly active and that multiple paradigms—sequence-based, graph-based, and hybrid—coexist. It also provides a conceptual bridge to the use of large language models (LLMs) for code: if code is "natural" in a statistical sense, then models trained on large corpora of code may be able to reason about security-relevant patterns. CipherMate's use of LLMs for explanation, remediation generation, and context-aware filtering aligns with this trend, while the hybrid architecture—combining rule-based static analysis with AI augmentation—acknowledges that learned models are most effective when grounded in structured, interpretable detection.

Large Language Models, Code Generation, and Security

The rise of large language models for code has introduced new opportunities and risks for security.

Pearce et al. (2022) conducted a landmark study in "Asleep at the keyboard? Assessing the security of GitHub Copilot's code contributions," presented at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (Oakland), one of the premier venues in computer security. They systematically evaluated whether Copilot—an AI code assistant trained on public GitHub repositories—generates secure or vulnerable code when developers write prompts that could lead to common vulnerabilities. The authors created 89 scenarios spanning SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS), insecure cryptographic usage (e.g., non-random IVs), and other OWASP-relevant issues. They found that Copilot produced vulnerable code in approximately 40% of cases, with particularly high rates for certain vulnerability types. The work established that LLMs, when used for code generation without guardrails, can amplify security risks by suggesting code that appears plausible but is insecure. For the present thesis, Pearce et al.'s findings have two implications. First, they underscore the danger of deploying LLMs for unchecked code generation in security-sensitive contexts. Second, they implicitly suggest that LLMs, when used for analysis rather than generation—and when their output is validated rather than applied blindly—could support security: explaining existing vulnerabilities, suggesting fixes for identified issues, and filtering false positives in static analysis output. CipherMate uses LLMs in this supporting role: for natural-language Q&A over scan results ("what should I fix first?"), for remediation generation (suggested patches that developers review before applying), and for context-aware filtering (assessing whether findings are in test code or production). Pearce et al.'s work thus informs both the opportunity (LLMs can understand and reason about security issues) and the necessity of constraining and validating their use.

Nijkamp et al. (2023) presented CodeGen in "CodeGen: An open large language model for code with multi-turn program synthesis," published at ICML. CodeGen demonstrated that large-scale, decoder-only models can perform multi-turn program synthesis. While CodeGen itself is a generative model, its existence and open release have enabled downstream applications in code understanding and analysis. CipherMate's AI layer can leverage such models (via OpenRouter, OpenAI, Anthropic, or local models like those served by Ollama) for the tasks of explanation and remediation. The literature on CodeGen and similar models provides evidence that LLMs have sufficient capacity for code-related reasoning; the present research investigates whether that capacity improves vulnerability detection and remediation when integrated with static analysis.

Standards, Frameworks, and Vulnerability Management

The OWASP Top Ten (OWASP Foundation, 2021), the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) maintained by MITRE (2023), and the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) programme (CVE Program, 2024) provide standardised taxonomies that are widely adopted in industry and academia. The National Vulnerability Database (NVD, 2024), operated by NIST, indexes CVE entries with severity scores, affected products, and references. These resources are authoritative for vulnerability classification and underpin CipherMate's dependency scanner—which checks packages against known CVEs—and its CVE enrichment of findings. They also provide the vocabulary for AI-generated explanations and remediation: mapping findings to CWE and CVE helps developers understand risk, compare with industry benchmarks, and prioritise effectively. Semgrep and Bandit, the primary code-pattern analyzers integrated into CipherMate, produce output that can be aligned with these taxonomies. The literature survey would be incomplete without acknowledging that CipherMate's contribution is not in redefining these standards but in applying them more effectively through AI-augmented tooling—reducing noise, clarifying meaning, and accelerating remediation—while remaining interoperable with existing vulnerability management workflows.

Synthesis and Relation to CipherMate

The literature reveals a consistent pattern: static analysis is powerful in theory but underused in practice due to false positives, poor presentation of findings, and lack of actionable remediation. AI and machine learning have shown promise for vulnerability detection (VulDeePecker, Devign) but also fragility under realistic evaluation (Chakraborty et al.). LLMs can assist with code understanding but may introduce new risks when used for generation (Pearce et al.). Developer studies (Johnson, Christakis & Bird, Sadowski) emphasise workflow integration, actionability, and trust.

CipherMate builds upon this body of work in several ways. First, it adopts a hybrid architecture: static analyzers provide detection; AI augments interpretation and action. This avoids the pitfalls of learned-only detection while leveraging AI for tasks where it adds value. Second, it directly addresses the barriers identified by Johnson et al. and Christakis & Bird: context-aware filtering to reduce false positives, exploitability scoring for prioritisation, natural-language Q&A for accessibility, and AI-generated remediation for actionability. Third, it implements the workflow-integration lessons of Sadowski et al. by embedding analysis in the IDE and supporting conversational interaction. Fourth, it uses LLMs for explanation and remediation—not for untrusted code generation—thereby aligning with the cautious stance implied by Pearce et al.

The present research extends prior work by evaluating whether these design choices yield measurable improvements in vulnerability detection (e.g., effective precision and recall after AI filtering) and remediation efficiency (e.g., time-to-patch, developer confidence, fix acceptance rate). To the author's knowledge, no prior system has combined multi-scanner static analysis (dependency, secrets, code-pattern, and smart-contract) with a conversational AI layer in an IDE extension and subjected that combination to a formal evaluation framework. CipherMate and its evaluation thus contribute both a system design and an empirical lens for assessing AI-augmented static analysis in real-world development contexts. The literature survey has identified the key papers that motivate and constrain this work: Johnson et al. and Christakis & Bird on developer needs, Sadowski et al. on industrial deployment, Li et al. and Zhou et al. on AI for vulnerability detection, Chakraborty et al. on evaluation rigour, Pearce et al. on LLM security implications, and Bessey et al. on the fundamental constraints of static analysis. CipherMate is positioned to build upon these contributions while addressing their limitations through a carefully designed hybrid architecture.
