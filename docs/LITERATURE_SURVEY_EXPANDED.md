# AI-Augmented Static Analysis for Vulnerability Detection and Developer Remediation Efficiency

## Abstract

Static application security testing promises to identify defects before software reaches production, yet empirical studies consistently show that organisations struggle to translate raw findings into sustained security improvements. Developers face alert fatigue from false positives, unclear prioritisation, and remediation guidance that does not fit local context. In parallel, machine learning and large language models (LLMs) have been proposed to rank alerts, explain vulnerabilities, and even suggest patches—but learned detectors can overstate accuracy under flawed evaluation protocols, and LLM-generated code may introduce new weaknesses when applied without review. This literature survey synthesises peer-reviewed and authoritative sources across three connected areas: the adoption and usability limits of traditional static analysis; neural and graph-based approaches to vulnerability detection together with critical reassessments of their validity; and the security implications of LLMs when used for code generation versus assistive explanation. The survey situates these threads within **CipherMate**, a Visual Studio Code–oriented assistant that combines conventional scanners (dependencies, secrets, code-pattern rules) with a conversational AI layer for interpretation, triage support, and human-reviewed fix suggestions. The stated direction of the project is to shrink the long-standing **detection–remediation gap** by improving signal quality and workflow integration while preserving auditable detection logic and non-authoritative use of generative models. Anticipated outcomes include a defensible hybrid architecture, awareness of evaluation pitfalls drawn from the software-engineering and security literature, and groundwork for empirical measurement of remediation efficiency. Training and fine-tuning experiments for security-oriented model components are expected to rely on **the school’s GPU computing infrastructure**, as consumer hardware is insufficient for repeatable training runs at thesis scale.

**Keywords** — static analysis; vulnerability detection; developer experience; large language models; software security; human–computer interaction; GPU computing; CipherMate

## 1. Introduction

Organisations continue to invest in static analysis because it can surface classes of bugs and vulnerabilities that elude casual review, yet the security benefit realised in practice often falls short of the theoretical maximum. The disconnect is not usually a lack of algorithms but a **human factors** problem: tools emit volumes of warnings, many of which are false or low value; messages are tied to rule IDs rather than to developer mental models; and remediation steps are generic or absent. When developers lose trust or cannot see how a finding relates to shipping code, they ignore or disable checks, and the organisational control weakens. The **CipherMate** project responds to this situation by treating static analysis as a **first-class input** to an IDE-integrated assistant that also leverages LLMs for natural-language explanation, prioritisation dialogue, and suggested fixes that remain subject to human approval. The central **goal**, stated plainly from the outset, is **not** to replace mature analyzers with a single opaque neural model whose mistakes are hard to audit, but to **augment** existing detection with AI-mediated interpretation and workflow support so that more findings turn into verified repairs.

This document reviews literature that justifies that design stance. It first summarises why static tools succeed or fail in real teams, drawing on large-scale and interview-based software-engineering studies. It then surveys learning-based vulnerability detection, including graph neural approaches and the methodological critique that recalibrated performance expectations. It next covers LLM security studies that caution against unchecked code generation while still allowing assistive roles. It closes the review with standards-based classification (CWE, CVE, NVD) and a synthesis of the **research gap** between detection and durable remediation. A **Resources** section follows, which explicitly includes **access to the school’s GPU systems** for **training and fine-tuning** experiments that exceed what a laptop can support reliably. The **Conclusion** connects these sources to evaluation strategy—for example, how survey or interview instruments might probe trust and actionability in line with prior developer studies. Together, the introduction and review frame CipherMate as an evidence-informed attempt to operationalise “shift left” in a way developers can actually sustain.

## 2. Summary of direction and anticipated outcomes

CipherMate is intentionally architected as a **hybrid system**. On one side, deterministic scanners and rule engines produce structured findings with traceable provenance; on the other, an LLM-backed conversational layer helps the developer ask follow-up questions, compare competing priorities, and obtain draft remediations that must pass review and testing before merge. This split reflects a consistent lesson from the literature: **detection** and **remediation support** are different problems, and collapsing them into one black box—especially a learned detector trained on imperfect corpora—invites both technical and organisational failure. The **direction** of the work is therefore to narrow the **detection–remediation gap** by improving clarity, relevance, and timing of feedback inside the developer’s primary workspace (the IDE), rather than by claiming omniscient automation.

**Anticipated outcomes** span engineering deliverables and evaluation awareness. At the engineering level, the project aims for a working extension demonstrating multi-scanner orchestration plus chat-based guidance grounded in actual tool output. At the evaluation level, it aims to adopt metrics and study designs sensitive to **false positive burden**, **time-to-remediate**, and **perceived usefulness**, and to avoid the inflated benchmarks criticised in recent deep-learning-for-vulnerability work. If user studies or surveys are conducted, instruments should reflect themes from classic developer studies—trust in warnings, clarity of messaging, and fit with existing habits. A further **anticipated outcome** on the machine-learning track is a **fine-tuned or adapter-based security-coding model** or comparable experiment; executing that track **requires the school’s GPU cluster or laboratory GPU allocation** for training and fine-tuning runs that would be impractically slow or unstable on CPU-only hardware. The survey document itself does not prove those outcomes; it establishes the conceptual and empirical basis on which they should be judged.

## 3. Literature review

### 3.1 Static analysis: capabilities and limitations

Static analysis inspects source code (and sometimes binaries or models) without executing the program, searching for patterns associated with defects, policy violations, or known vulnerability classes. Industry experience summarised by Bessey *et al.* [1] shows that mature tools can operate at enormous scale but only where **precision**, **performance**, and **usability** are engineered seriously; otherwise adoption stalls. Johnson *et al.* [2], in a widely cited ICSE study, found that developers often abandon or ignore static tools when confronted with **false positives** and with warnings that lack actionable framing—so accuracy and presentation are jointly decisive. Christakis and Bird [3] reinforce that developers prefer **smaller sets of high-confidence issues** over exhaustive but noisy lists, which implies that maximising raw detection count is misaligned with practitioner goals. Sadowski *et al.* [4] describe Google’s lessons: analysis must be **embedded in workflow**, fast enough not to block flow, and tied to clear next steps; otherwise engineers rationalise ignoring alerts. Muske and Serebrenik [5] systematically review alarm-handling techniques—filtering, ranking, clustering, and automated fix suggestion—and note trade-offs between rigid heuristics and context sensitivity. Empirical studies of open source by Beller *et al.* [6] show that static tools are common but often run in **default configurations**, suggesting that low setup cost dominates deep customisation. Nadi *et al.* [7] observe that even CI integration does not guarantee engagement if developers still perceive output as low signal. **CipherMate** is positioned to respond to this body of work by keeping proven scanners as the source of truth for *what* was found, while using AI to help with *how* to understand and order findings inside the IDE—directly targeting trust, presentation, and workflow fit rather than pretending that more rules alone solve adoption.

### 3.2 Machine learning for vulnerability detection

Researchers have explored neural models that classify code fragments or whole functions as vulnerable or benign, often reporting high accuracy on curated benchmarks. Li *et al.* [8] introduced VulDeePecker, using “code gadgets” and recurrent networks to capture flow-sensitive cues for C/C++ API misuse, and reported strong results on security-relevant datasets. Zhou *et al.* [9] proposed Devign, combining abstract syntax, control flow, and data flow into graph neural networks to better capture program semantics; their work illustrated that **structure-aware** representations can outperform naive sequence models on real-world C code. These contributions established that learning from code can surface patterns that hand-written rules miss. However, Chakraborty *et al.* [10] subjected similar approaches to stricter methodology, exposing **data leakage**, unrealistic label distributions, and **artifact learning** (models exploiting dataset idiosyncrasies rather than generalisable vulnerability semantics); under corrected conditions, performance dropped sharply. That critique is central for any project that might later train custom models: **evaluation hygiene** and held-out realism matter as much as architecture. Allamanis *et al.* [11] survey machine learning on “big code,” linking code regularities to natural-language methods and foreshadowing large-scale generative models—while also noting dataset bias and unpredictable behaviour on out-of-distribution projects. Ribeiro *et al.* [12] demonstrate that learning to **rank** static-analysis warnings can improve developer throughput, which supports using ML on **outputs of existing tools** rather than as a sole oracle. For **CipherMate**, the implication is to privilege **assistive** ML—ranking, filtering, explaining—grounded in scanner output, and to use **school GPU resources** when training or fine-tuning such components so experiments are reproducible and comparable across thesis milestones.

### 3.3 Large language models in security contexts

Large language models have rapidly become default infrastructure for code completion, translation, and documentation, but security research warns against treating their output as production-ready without review. Pearce *et al.* [13] evaluated GitHub Copilot–style generation across many scenarios inspired by common vulnerability classes and found that a substantial fraction of completions were **insecure**, even when prompts were mundane. That result has two readings for tool builders: first, **unconstrained auto-application** of LLM-generated patches in security-sensitive code is hazardous; second, the same models often excel at **natural-language explanation** and structured reasoning when outputs are verified by humans and anchored to concrete artifacts (e.g. scanner traces). CipherMate therefore adopts a **consultative** role for the LLM—summarising findings, comparing options, drafting patches for diff review—while leaving **authoritative detection** to rule-based analysis whose behaviour can be inspected and regression-tested. This division mitigates the worst failure mode Pearce *et al.* identify (silent introduction of vulnerabilities through “helpful” code) while still exploiting fluency and context synthesis. Future work may include **fine-tuning** a smaller security-focused model for explanation and remediation style; such runs are **not** optional on a laptop for serious iteration and are planned on **the institution’s GPU systems** under approved usage policies, in addition to API-based frontier models for comparison.

### 3.4 Standards and vulnerability classification

Effective communication across security, development, and operations teams depends on **shared taxonomies** so that findings can be exchanged, aggregated, and compared across tools and vendors. The Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) [14] catalogues types of software weaknesses; the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) programme [15] names specific disclosed issues; and the National Vulnerability Database (NVD) [16] enriches CVE records with severity metrics and affected product metadata. These resources allow CipherMate’s dependency and pattern scanners to speak the same language as enterprise vulnerability management processes and to align reports with audit and compliance artefacts that auditors already recognise. At the same time, practitioners increasingly recognise that **numeric severity alone**—for example, a base CVSS score—does not determine operational priority: context such as exposure, asset value, authentication boundaries, and compensating controls reshapes real risk in ways a global score cannot encode. A tool that merely sorts by CVSS therefore duplicates dashboards developers already distrust and may even amplify cynicism toward automated output. CipherMate’s AI layer can instead support **contextual triage** when grounded in repository facts (e.g. whether code is deployed, handles credentials, or sits behind additional controls), always subject to human judgment and local policy. Standards thus provide **interoperability and vocabulary**; the assistant’s value is in **situated interpretation**, not in replacing organisational risk decisions or accountability structures.

### 3.5 Synthesis and research gap

Taken together, the surveyed strands point to a **structural gap**: static analysis and learned detectors have improved **how much** can be flagged, but they have not, on their own, closed the loop to **reliable remediation** at scale. Developer studies explain disengagement through trust and usability failures [2]–[4]; ML papers show both promise and **fragility** under honest evaluation [10]; LLM studies warn that generation without guardrails worsens security [13] while still supporting explanatory tasks. Few integrated systems combine **multi-scanner detection**, **IDE-native workflow**, **conversational clarification**, and **reviewable fix assistance** in one coherent product narrative aimed at mainstream developers. CipherMate targets that integration explicitly. The research gap also extends to **rigorous evaluation** of such hybrids—metrics must reflect false-positive burden, fix quality after review, and time cost, not only precision-recall on synthetic corpora. Where custom models are trained, **school-provided GPUs** are part of the **minimum viable experimental setup** for training and fine-tuning at thesis depth; without them, claims about model behaviour would rest on ad hoc local runs that are hard to reproduce or compare.

## 5. Conclusion and evaluation

The literature reviewed here converges on a pragmatic conclusion: **finding** vulnerabilities is necessary but insufficient; **sustained security improvement** depends on whether developers understand findings, trust them enough to act, and have feasible paths to verified repair. Static analysis research stresses false positives, warning design, and workflow integration [1]–[4]; machine learning research offers ranking and detection ideas but demands **rigorous evaluation** to avoid overclaiming [10]; LLM research shows that **generation** and **assistance** carry different risk profiles [13]. CipherMate’s hybrid design—scanners plus conversational, non-authoritative AI support—is aligned with that evidence rather than with hype-driven full automation.

For **evaluation**, the project should measure outcomes that mirror the literature’s concerns: for example, reduction in perceived noise, time from alert to merged fix, and qualitative themes from surveys or interviews (trust, clarity, cognitive load). Instruments can borrow constructs from Johnson *et al.* [2] and Christakis and Bird [3] rather than inventing ad hoc Likert scales in isolation. If custom models are evaluated, benchmarks must avoid the leakage patterns Chakraborty *et al.* [10] describe, and training procedures should be **reproducible**—which, again, presupposes **documented use of the school’s GPU systems** for training and fine-tuning rather than one-off laptop runs. In sum, the surveyed work supplies both **motivation** (the detection–remediation gap) and **constraints** (trust, evaluation honesty, LLM guardrails) that should govern how CipherMate is built, evaluated, and reported in the remainder of the thesis.
